# Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative a person gives up when they choose one option over another. Marginal Revolution University illustrates it with two people, Bob and Ann, each able to spend their time either gathering bananas or catching fish. Bob can gather 10 bananas or catch 10 fish in a given period; for him, one banana costs one fish. Ann can gather 10 bananas or catch 30 fish; for her, one banana costs three fish. Ann's opportunity cost of a banana is higher than Bob's, purely because fishing is so much more productive for her. That difference in opportunity cost, not any difference in skill at gathering bananas, is what makes trade between them worthwhile: if Ann focuses on fish and Bob on bananas, both end up with more of both than if each tried to be self-sufficient.

Russell Roberts, writing for Econlib, extends the idea past textbook examples: "to think like an economist, you have to know what you're giving up in order to get something else." Even a free lunch has an opportunity cost, the time spent listening rather than doing something else, and the classic case of college tuition is not the biggest cost of a degree; the foregone wages from the job you could have had instead usually are. The same logic runs through the module's rose example. When rose prices rose after the 1973 oil shock, the opportunity cost of buying roses rose with them: the same money now bought fewer roses, or the equivalent value in something else entirely. Every consumer's decision, buy the roses and spend less elsewhere, or buy something else, was really a decision about where that money's opportunity cost was lowest for them.

Opportunity cost is forward-looking. It asks what you are giving up by choosing this option now, not what you have already spent trying to get here, which is a different question covered by sunk costs elsewhere in this module.

## Why this matters in policy

Government budgets record what a programme costs in outlay, not what it costs in opportunity. A programme that consumes scarce administrative capacity, crowds out private investment, or absorbs political attention that could have gone elsewhere carries a cost well beyond its line item, and that cost is systematically invisible in standard accounting. Naming the foregone alternative, what else that money, capacity, or attention could have achieved, is one of the simplest and most under-used disciplines in policy evaluation.
