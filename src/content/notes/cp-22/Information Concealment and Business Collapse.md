# Information Concealment and Business Collapse

In 1992, Britain's oldest merchant bank, Barings, hired a derivatives trader named Nick Leeson. Leeson favoured large, speculative, unauthorised trades, and when they went well the bank rewarded him with a £180,000 bonus. When they went badly, he concealed the losses instead of reporting them. By the time he had built a position that depended on Japan's stock market staying calm overnight, hours before the Kobe earthquake, his hidden losses had grown from £208 million to around £862 million. He fled, leaving a note that read simply "I'm sorry." Barings was declared insolvent within days, brought down not by the original bad trades but by the year of concealment that let them compound unchecked. Had management known about the £208 million loss when it was £208 million, they had options. By the time they found out, they had none.

Nokia's collapse looks nothing like Barings on the surface, but the mechanism is the same. By 2005, roughly one in three mobiles sold worldwide was a Nokia. When the iPhone arrived, Nokia's market share fell 90% in six years, and Microsoft bought what remained in 2013. According to a 2016 report on the collapse, Nokia's own engineers and developers knew that its operating system, Symbian, was years behind Apple's iOS. Middle managers, however, were not confident enough to relay that assessment to senior leadership, and Nokia's top executives, unlike Apple's, contained few people technically equipped to spot the problem themselves even if it had reached them. The company did not fail because nobody knew it had a problem. It failed because the people who knew could not, or would not, make the people with the authority to act aware of it.

Both cases share a structure worth naming explicitly: a gap between operational knowledge (what is actually happening on a trading floor, or inside an engineering team) and decision-making authority (who has the power to correct course). When that gap is filled by concealment or by institutional cowardice, small, correctable problems have time to become large, uncorrectable ones.

## Why this matters in policy

Public institutions have the same operational-knowledge-to-authority gap, and the same incentives for the people closest to a problem to under-report it rather than be blamed for it. Whistleblower protections, routine external audit, and cultures that reward early bad news rather than punishing the messenger are not bureaucratic overhead; they are the direct institutional analogue of what Barings and Nokia both lacked.
