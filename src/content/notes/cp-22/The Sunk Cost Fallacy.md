# The Sunk Cost Fallacy

A sunk cost is an expenditure that has already happened and cannot be recovered, whatever decision is made next. Russell Roberts's example is a car that breaks down: if you pay to fix it and it breaks down again, the decision to fix it a second time should be made purely on whether the second repair is worth it, not on how much you have already spent. "I've already lost the money on the first repair," he writes. Bringing the size of that first repair bill into the second decision is the sunk cost fallacy, and it is one of the most common and best-documented errors in economic reasoning.

The mechanism is straightforward once named: people find it painful to treat past spending as irrelevant, because doing so feels like admitting the earlier spending was wasted. So instead of asking "is this investment worth continuing, evaluated fresh, from today," decision-makers ask "how do I avoid having wasted what I already put in," and end up throwing good money after bad to avoid that admission. The economically correct question and the psychologically comfortable question point in different directions, and the comfortable one usually wins.

The fallacy is especially dangerous in government policy, for a reason private firms face less acutely. A public infrastructure project, a subsidy scheme, or a military commitment that has already consumed public funds acquires a political constituency almost as soon as the money is spent: contractors, beneficiaries, and officials whose judgement is on the line. Reversing course then reads publicly as an admission of failure, which creates pressure to continue a bad policy well past the point where continuation is clearly worse than stopping. The sunk cost is not just an accounting distraction here, it becomes a political commitment device that makes rational abandonment nearly impossible.

## Why this matters in policy

"We've already spent X on this" is not, by itself, a reason to spend more. It is a fact about the past that a good decision-maker sets aside to ask the only question that matters: given where things stand today, is continuing the best use of the next rupee? Building review points into large public programmes, ones that force this question to be asked explicitly and separately from the sunk cost, is a practical defence against a bias that is otherwise very hard for any individual official to resist alone.
