# Markets

This module is about the everyday machinery of a market economy: what a price actually communicates, what it enables people to do without anyone directing them, and what happens when businesses ignore the signals in front of them. The unifying idea, borrowed from Friedrich Hayek, is that no central authority can hold all the knowledge needed to allocate resources well. Prices are the mechanism that lets dispersed, local knowledge add up to global coordination without anyone in charge.

The case material is a mix of teaching classics (the 1973 oil shock and the Valentine's Day rose trade, Bob and Ann trading bananas for fish) and real corporate history (Barings, Nokia, Toys "R" Us, Thomas Cook, and the Titanic's rivets). Together they show the same logic working, and failing, in very different settings.

Topics in this module:

- **Prices as signals**: what a price conveys, and what happens when the signal is suppressed by controls or distorted by an externality.
- **How prices allocate resources**: why scarcity is the problem prices exist to solve, and how supply adjusts over time.
- **Producer and consumer response to the oil shock**: the Kenyan rose farm and Valentine's Day chocolate substitution, in two parts.
- **Decentralised coordination and the invisible hand**: how millions of independent decisions add up without communication.
- **Hayek and the knowledge problem**: why a central planner can never gather what the market already knows.
- **The price system as emergent order**: the price mechanism as something nobody invented and everybody depends on.
- **Opportunity cost**: the cost that never appears on an invoice.
- **The sunk cost fallacy, in theory and in practice**: the Titanic, Toys "R" Us, and why "we've already spent this much" is the wrong sentence in a budget meeting.
- **Information concealment and business collapse**: Barings and Nokia, and what happens when bad news doesn't travel upward.
- **When businesses miss the signal**: Ratner's own words, the 2008 belief in ever-rising house prices, and Thomas Cook's failure to notice the internet.
