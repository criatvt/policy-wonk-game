# Consumer Response to the Oil Shock

While entrepreneurs were solving the rose-price problem from the supply side, ordinary consumers were solving it from the demand side, without any coordination between the two groups. When rose prices rose because of higher oil costs, buyers faced a genuine choice at the margin: pay more and get the same roses, buy fewer roses and spend the difference elsewhere, or substitute something else entirely. Chocolate and teddy bears became more attractive Valentine's Day gifts precisely because roses had become relatively more expensive. Very few people abandoned gift-giving altogether. Most people adjusted quantity, or swapped, or simply paid up, depending on how much they valued roses specifically compared with the alternatives.

This is what economists mean by a marginal decision. Nobody was choosing between "roses" and "no roses" in the abstract. They were choosing, at a specific higher price, whether one more rose was worth what it now cost compared with the next-best thing they could do with that money. Some people's answer was yes. Some people's answer was chocolate.

The interesting feature of this is how much variation it produced, and how little of it had to be planned. Different consumers, with different tastes and different budgets, made different choices in response to the exact same price signal, and every one of those choices was a rational response given the person making it. Multiply that variation across millions of buyers and the aggregate effect, some substitution toward chocolate, some reduction in quantity, some unchanged spending, is a rich and largely unpredictable pattern that nonetheless adds up to an efficient market-wide adjustment to a real cost increase.

## Why this matters in policy

Policies that assume a single, uniform consumer response to a price change (a subsidy will be "passed through," a tax will change behaviour by exactly X per cent) tend to undersell how much real variation exists in how people actually respond. Different households have different substitutes available to them, different budgets, and different attachments to the good in question. Modelling demand response well means taking that variation seriously rather than treating consumers as one representative buyer with one representative reaction.
