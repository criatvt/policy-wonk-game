# How Prices Allocate Resources

The fundamental economic problem is not production, it is allocation: how to distribute a finite set of resources across competing, unlimited wants. There is no committee that solves this every morning. Prices do it continuously, through a simple feedback loop. A rising price signals that a good is becoming more valued relative to the resources it takes to make it, which pulls in more production and investment. A falling price signals the opposite, and resources drift toward better uses elsewhere. This is what economists mean when they say prices "discover" where resources are most valued: not that anyone searches for the answer, but that the search happens automatically as producers follow the incentive.

The mechanism is also self-correcting over the longer run. A price rise creates a profit opportunity, which draws in new supply. As supply increases, the price falls back, at least until conditions change again. This is exactly what happened after the 1973 oil shock: oil-heated greenhouses in New Jersey became expensive to run, rose prices in the United States climbed, and the higher price attracted entrepreneurs into rose production in Kenya and Ecuador, where the climate did the heating job for free. Within a few years, supply had adjusted and the shortage the original price spike signalled had been addressed, not by decree, but by thousands of independent people responding to the same number.

None of this requires a central authority to declare what should be produced where. It requires only that prices are free to move, and that people are free to act on them. Historically, all attempts to replace this feedback with administrative allocation, deciding in advance which factory produces what, in what quantity, and for whom, have struggled to match it, because the allocation problem the market solves passively is genuinely hard to solve on purpose.

## Why this matters in policy

Whenever policy tries to allocate a scarce resource directly, water in a drought, hospital beds, subsidised credit, it is attempting by administrative rule what prices normally do by feedback. That can be the right call on distributional grounds. But it is worth being honest that the substitute mechanism, whether a queue, a quota, or a means test, has to do the same job the price signal was doing, usually with far less information about who values the resource most and at what moment. Good non-price rationing design starts by asking what information the price mechanism was supplying, and how the replacement will supply it instead.
