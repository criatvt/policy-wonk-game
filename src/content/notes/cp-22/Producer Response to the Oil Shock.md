# Producer Response to the Oil Shock

Before the 1970s oil shock, most roses sold in the United States were grown in heated greenhouses in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. When the price of oil rose sharply, heating those greenhouses became far more expensive. That cost increase shifted the supply curve for flowers upward and pushed rose prices higher too. On its own, that would just be a story about one input cost feeding through to one product price. What makes it interesting is what happened next.

Seeing the higher price of oil and the higher price of roses, entrepreneurs started to think about producing flowers without heating a greenhouse at all. Kenya and Ecuador have year-round natural warmth. Farmers there could grow roses without spending anything on heating fuel. Entrepreneurs encouraged Kenyan and Ecuadorian growers to start planting roses, and invested in the transport infrastructure, refrigerated trucks, air freight, auction networks, needed to get flowers from those fields to Valentine's Day bouquets in Chicago within days. This is the same shift in comparative advantage the module's opportunity-cost material describes: once the cost of one input rose, the cheapest way to produce roses was no longer in New Jersey, it was wherever the sun did the heating for free.

Nobody planned this outcome, and nobody could have. The entrepreneurs who moved into Kenyan and Ecuadorian rose farming were not instructed to solve America's oil-price problem. They were following a profit opportunity created by a price signal, and their pursuit of that opportunity happened to free up American oil for uses, like jet fuel, where there were fewer easy substitutes. The reallocation of a scarce resource away from a low-value use (heating greenhouses) and toward a higher-value use (uses with no easy substitute) happened through the ordinary self-interest of producers responding to a price, not through central direction.

## Why this matters in policy

Industrial policy that tries to pick winners in advance is, in effect, trying to do what the rose-oil story shows markets doing without being told: reallocate production toward its lowest-cost location. The rose story is a caution about how confidently that can be done from a desk. Nobody in 1973 could have predicted that flowers grown in Kenya would become the answer to an American energy problem. The entrepreneurs who found that answer did so by chasing a price signal that pointed them there, not by being asked the right question by a planning committee.
