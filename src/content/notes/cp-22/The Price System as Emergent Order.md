# The Price System as Emergent Order

Nobody could have predicted, in 1973, that an oil shock would end with Kenyan roses on American doorsteps and more chocolate on Valentine's Day shelves. That is not a failure of forecasting. It is a structural feature of how the price system works: the specific outcomes it produces are not knowable in advance, because they depend on information (a particular farmer's land, a particular consumer's tastes, a particular entrepreneur's idea) that is scattered across millions of people and never assembled anywhere. The Nobel laureate Vernon Smith described the pricing system as "a scientific mystery as deep, fundamental, and inspiring as that of the expanding universe or the forces that bind matter." That is a large claim for something as mundane as a supermarket price tag, and it is meant to be. The mechanism aggregates more information than any person or institution could hold, and it does so without anyone administering it.

"I, Rose," a short teaching essay modelled on Leonard Read's classic "I, Pencil," makes the same point about a single Valentine's Day bouquet. A rose bought in Chicago in February passed through a farmer, a shipper, an auctioneer, and a retailer, most of them on different continents, cooperating to move a fragile, perishable product from field to hand in days. No single person in that chain understands the whole process. There is no "rose tsar." Each person sees only their own part and acts on their own interest, and the coordination happens anyway, guided, in Adam Smith's phrase, by an invisible hand.

The deeper implication is about limits, not just marvels. If the rose supply chain, or the response to an oil shock, cannot be planned by any single mind because the relevant knowledge is too dispersed, then attempts to plan comparably complex systems from the centre, whether an entire industrial sector or a national supply chain, face the same structural problem. The scale of the ambition does not create the missing knowledge.

## Why this matters in policy

Industrial policy and supply-chain strategy are popular tools precisely because the outcomes they promise (a domestic semiconductor industry, a resilient supply chain, self-sufficiency in a strategic good) sound achievable by sufficiently determined planning. The rose and pencil examples are a standing caution: coordinating complex, multi-actor systems well usually requires harnessing dispersed information, not overriding it, and a planning body that dispenses with prices dispenses with its main source of that information too.
