# When Businesses Miss the Signal

Some business failures do not involve hiding information from anyone. They involve a company either destroying its own signal, misreading a signal the whole market shares, or failing to notice that a signal has changed entirely.

Gerald Ratner's jewellery chain is the clearest case of self-destruction. At a 1991 speech to the Institute of Directors, Ratner joked that a set of his cut-glass decanters was "total crap," meant as a laugh about how cheap they were. The joke reached a newspaper reporter in the room, and the public reaction was immediate: the low price on Ratner's goods had always been the signal that they were good value, not worthless, and Ratner's own words broke that link in front of everyone at once. His company lost around £500 million in value within days. Nothing about the products had changed. The signal had.

The 2008 financial crisis is closer to the opposite problem: not one person destroying a true signal, but a great many people sharing a false one. In the years before the crash, a widespread belief took hold that property prices only ever rise, described in the Economist's account as a "magical belief" that persisted despite ample warning. Financial institutions built enormous, interlinked positions on the assumption that this belief was reliable. When it turned out not to be, the collapse of the belief, not any single concealed fact, froze Western banking for months. Lehman Brothers, founded in 1847 and considered too important to fail, filed for bankruptcy anyway.

Thomas Cook shows a third pattern: a firm that simply stopped updating its read of the market it operated in. The company that invented mass tourism in 1841 built its entire value around being the intermediary who could book hotels and flights on a customer's behalf. The internet made that intermediary role available to anyone with a browser, and by the time Thomas Cook tried to relaunch as a purely online business in 2019, saddled with £1.2 billion of debt from an earlier merger, it had waited too long to adapt to a signal, the rise of direct booking, that had been visible for the better part of two decades.

## Why this matters in policy

Three different failure modes, a destroyed signal, a shared false belief, and a stale read of a changed environment, all point to the same discipline: institutions, public and private, need routine, honest mechanisms for checking whether the signals they are relying on are still true, still shared correctly, and still current, rather than assuming that what worked yesterday will keep working today.
