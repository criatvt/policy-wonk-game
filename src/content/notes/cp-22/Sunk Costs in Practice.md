# Sunk Costs in Practice

When the wreck of the Titanic was explored in the 1990s, scientists found that its three million wrought-iron rivets contained unusually high levels of slag, the glassy residue of iron smelting, which made them brittle in cold Atlantic water. The riveting had already happened, the ship had already been built, and by the time the flaw mattered it could not be undone: a textbook sunk cost, incurred long before the disaster it contributed to. The lesson is not really about rivets. It is that quality problems built into a decision early on often stay hidden until the moment they become catastrophic, well after there is anything anyone can do about the original spending that caused them.

Toys "R" Us shows the fallacy operating on a modern balance sheet. Burdened by debt and unable to compete with rivals who were investing in in-store experience (Build-a-Bear's toys assembled in front of customers, Lego's hands-on brick tables), the company chose a $50 million partnership that redirected its own website traffic to Amazon rather than investing in its own online capability. It was, in effect, doubling down on an existing weak position instead of writing it off and rebuilding, and by the time the Amazon deal ended badly in 2006, Toys "R" Us had lost years it could have spent building the online presence it eventually needed anyway. It filed for bankruptcy in 2017.

The Economist's essay draws a common thread through Titanic and Evergrande, a Chinese property developer that built roughly 1,300 housing projects, a football team, and fifteen theme parks on $300 billion of borrowed money before defaulting in 2021: both cases show an audience, creditors, shareholders, or citizens, whose confidence depends on the appearance of continued strength. Reversing course, admitting the ship is taking on water or the company is insolvent, risks triggering the very panic that continuation was meant to avoid. That asymmetry, where honesty looks more dangerous than denial in the short run, is what lets sunk cost reasoning escalate all the way to collapse.

## Why this matters in policy

Public institutions face the same audience problem as Titanic's officers and Evergrande's directors: admitting a flagship project is failing looks, in the short run, more costly than continuing it. The Titanic and Evergrande cases argue for building early, low-stakes off-ramps into large commitments, so that correcting course does not have to look like a dramatic failure the first time it happens.
