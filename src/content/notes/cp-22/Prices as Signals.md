# Prices as Signals

A price signal is information about relative scarcity that gives people an incentive to act on it. When a price rises, it says that something has become scarcer or more valued than before, and it rewards anyone who responds, by conserving it, by producing more of it, or by finding a substitute. Marginal Revolution University's teaching video puts it simply: "a price is a signal wrapped up in an incentive." Nobody has to explain why the price moved. The movement itself carries the instruction.

Price also carries information beyond scarcity. It signals quality and the seller's own confidence in what they are selling, which is why undermining that signal can be so damaging. In 1991 the jeweller Gerald Ratner told an audience of 6,000 business people that one of his products, a silver-plated tray and decanter set selling for £4.95, was "total crap." He meant it as a joke about how cheap the item was. Instead, he destroyed the signal his own low prices had been sending: that the goods were good value, not worthless. The value of his company fell by roughly £500 million in the days that followed, and "doing a Ratner" became City shorthand for a self-inflicted act of economic harm. Price and quality are meant to travel together. Ratner's words severed that link in public, and the market punished it instantly.

## When the signal is suppressed: price controls and externalities

Governments sometimes intervene in the signal itself, usually with good intentions. Price controls that hold a price below its market-clearing level are a common example. The economics is unforgiving: suppressing the signal does not remove the underlying scarcity, it only removes suppliers' incentive to respond to it. The result is a shortage: the same unaffordability problem controls were meant to solve, reborn as unavailability, queues, and black markets.

The opposite failure is a missing signal. Market prices do not naturally include costs that fall on people outside the transaction, such as the climate cost of burning fossil fuels. A carbon price is an attempt to fix this by making the price of energy reflect its full social cost, not just its production cost. Getting this right is hard: the price has to be set close to the true social cost, applied broadly enough that production does not simply relocate to avoid it, and held steady long enough for producers and consumers to actually respond to it.

## Why this matters in policy

Most economic policy problems are, underneath, problems of getting prices to tell the truth. Price controls, subsidies, and unpriced externalities all change what the signal says, and the change in behaviour that follows is rarely the one intended. Reading a policy proposal by asking "what does this do to the signal, and who is left to respond to it" is a faster route to spotting unintended consequences than almost any other single question.
