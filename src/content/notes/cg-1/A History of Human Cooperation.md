# A History of Human Cooperation

The deck opens with a timeline that runs from the Big Bang, 13.5 billion years ago, through the formation of Earth, the emergence of the first organisms, and the evolution of the genus Homo in Africa some 2.5 million years ago. Within that long timeline, a handful of moments stand out because they changed not just what humans could do, but how humans could cooperate with each other.

The first is the Cognitive Revolution, roughly 70,000 years ago. This is when language and shared myths emerged, and with them the beginning of history as a record of collective human action. What made this a decisive evolutionary advantage was not raw intelligence but scale of cooperation: shared myths, from tribal ancestors to nations to companies, let sapiens organise in groups far larger than any that could hold together through personal acquaintance alone. This is the capability that let sapiens spread out of Africa, across Australia around 50,000 years ago, and across the Americas by roughly 16,000 years ago, eventually outcompeting the Neanderthals, who went extinct around 30,000 years ago.

The second turning point is the Agricultural Revolution, about 12,000 years ago, when humans domesticated plants and animals and settled rather than foraged. This laid the ground for the first kingdoms, writing systems and money around 5,000 years ago, and for the sequence of empires, religions and revolutions that follow: the Persian empire and coinage, the spread of Buddhism, the Han and Roman empires, Christianity, Islam, and eventually the Scientific Revolution and European conquest of the Americas and the oceans.

The third turning point is the Industrial Revolution, about 200 years ago. Its defining social change was that family and community, historically the primary institutions organising daily life, were substantially replaced by the state and the market. Work, welfare, education and social support increasingly ran through impersonal institutions rather than kin and neighbours.

## Why this matters in policy

Each of these turning points changed the unit of cooperation that policy has to work with: from bands and tribes bound by myth, to settled kingdoms bound by law and religion, to nation-states and markets bound by impersonal institutions. Understanding this history is a reminder that the state-and-market default we now treat as natural is itself a relatively recent cultural arrangement, not an inevitable end point. It also frames the present, where humans have begun to transcend Earth and reshape organisms through intelligent design, as simply the next turning point in the same long sequence.
