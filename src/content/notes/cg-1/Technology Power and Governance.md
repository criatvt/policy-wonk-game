# Technology Power and Governance

The deck frames the political stakes of technology through a direct question: does the spread of powerful technology lead toward technological authoritarianism, or toward regulation that keeps it in check? This is presented as a genuinely open outcome, not a foregone conclusion in either direction. Technologies that make surveillance, monitoring and behavioural tracking cheap and scalable can be used to extend state power deep into private life. The same technologies, under different institutional arrangements, can instead be brought under transparent rules that set limits on what is permitted.

Which outcome prevails is treated as a matter of governance choice, not technological inevitability. This connects to what the deck calls "history's lesson": every technology is a product of its economic and political context, and carries a program, agenda and philosophy that can be good or evil. None of this is neutral. All of it, the deck insists, therefore requires scrutiny, criticism and control, rather than being adopted on the strength of its technical performance alone.

This has a direct implication for technology transfer across borders. A technology developed in one economic and political context embeds assumptions about infrastructure, regulation and organisation that were shaped by that context. Moving the same technology into a different setting without adaptation can produce outcomes nobody intended, because the embedded assumptions do not automatically hold somewhere else.

## Why this matters in policy

The practical governance principle that follows is straightforward to state and hard to institutionalise: evaluating a technology purely on efficiency, safety or economic grounds misses what matters most, the political and ethical assumptions built into its design. A government that promotes a technically sound technology, backed by scientific consensus, can still see uptake collapse if the technology conflicts with a society's cultural values, no matter how large the subsidy. Sociocultural legitimacy, not just technical merit, is a prerequisite for technology to function as intended, which is precisely why scrutiny, criticism and control need to happen before deployment, not only after something has already gone wrong.
