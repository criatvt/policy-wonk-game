# Technology and the Problem of Subjectivity

The deck makes a pointed claim about what technology, understood as technique, wants: it wants to solve the "dilemma of subjectivity." Diversity, complexity, subjectivity and the ambiguity of human judgement are described as enemies of technique. This is a strong claim, and it is worth unpacking carefully rather than reading it as simple hostility to human beings.

Technical systems need standardisation and predictability to function at all. An engineering process, an algorithm, or an administrative procedure works by reducing inputs to consistent, comparable categories and applying consistent rules to them. Human judgement, by contrast, is diverse across individuals, context-dependent, and often genuinely ambiguous, the same situation reasonably interpreted in different ways by different people. From the standpoint of technique, this variability is not a feature to be respected but a source of friction to be engineered around.

The deck's most striking illustration of this is the claim that technology pushes psychology, sociology and anthropology to become "sciences." The point is not that empirical rigour in the social sciences is undesirable. It is that turning subjective human experience into quantifiable, predictable variables is itself a technological move, one that makes human behaviour more manageable as an input to technical systems, at the cost of losing whatever cannot be reduced to a variable in the first place.

## Why this matters in policy

Evidence-based policy leans heavily on quantification: measurable outcomes, controlled comparisons, standardised indicators. This is genuinely valuable, but the module's framing is a useful caution against assuming that whatever cannot be measured does not matter. Policies built entirely around metrics that technique can process well, cost per unit, response time, throughput, can systematically underweight the psychic, emotional and moral dimensions of a policy problem simply because those dimensions resist standardisation. Good policy design keeps a place for judgement that quantification cannot fully capture, rather than treating everything that cannot be measured as a rounding error.
