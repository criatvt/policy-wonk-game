# The Human-Machine Transition

The deck lays out a six-stage sequence that describes how the relationship between humans and machines has shifted over time: humans are not machines; humans are in some respects like machines; humans are machines; machines can be connected; machines can duplicate human intelligence; machines are sentient, with a question mark left deliberately attached to that last stage.

Read as a sequence rather than a list, it traces a boundary that keeps eroding. Each stage narrows the gap between what humans do and what machines can do, until the final stage asks whether the gap disappears altogether. The deck does not claim the last stage has been reached. It flags it as the live, unresolved question the sequence is heading toward.

This matters because human institutions, law, ethics, accountability, employment, are built on the earlier stages of this sequence. They assume a fairly clean separation between human actors, who have judgement, intent and legal personality, and machines, which are tools that humans use and remain responsible for. As machines take on more human-like capabilities, connecting to each other, duplicating aspects of human intelligence, and potentially exhibiting something like sentience, that clean separation becomes harder to maintain. The institutional frameworks built for the earlier stages do not automatically know what to do with the later ones.

## Why this matters in policy

As machines increasingly govern complex systems, from credit decisions to content moderation to logistics, the policy question shifts from "should we adopt this technology" to "how do we govern systems that now govern us." This exposes a real institutional gap: regulatory and legal frameworks were designed to oversee human actors with intent and accountability, not autonomous technical systems whose behaviour can be difficult to trace to any single decision-maker. Closing that gap, rather than simply debating whether machines can or cannot be sentient, is the practical governance challenge the sequence points toward.
