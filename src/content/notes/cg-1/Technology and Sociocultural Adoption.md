# Technology and Sociocultural Adoption

The deck defines technology as the way scientific knowledge is applied for practical purposes. It includes machines, such as computers, but also the techniques and processes behind them, such as how computer chips are actually produced. More broadly, technology is the making, using and knowing of tools, machines, techniques, crafts and systems of organisation that solve problems. It is application, not just discovery.

Being technically sound is not the same as being adopted. The deck sets out three phases a technology must pass through to become sociocultural accepted: awareness, where people learn that the technology exists; evaluation, where they assess it against their own needs, beliefs and circumstances; and adoption, where they actually take it up and integrate it into daily life. A technology can be well engineered and still fail if it stalls at the evaluation phase, because the people being asked to adopt it judge it as a poor fit for their values or circumstances.

This is why technical superiority is not a reliable predictor of success. A technology that is objectively better on the specifications that engineers care about can still be rejected if it conflicts with prevailing values, threatens established roles, or simply asks people to change habits faster or in directions they are not willing to go. Conversely, a technically modest technology can spread quickly if it fits smoothly into how a society already thinks and lives, requiring little cultural adjustment to take hold.

## Why this matters in policy

Government programmes routinely subsidise or mandate the adoption of technologies that experts consider clearly beneficial, from clean cookstoves to digital payments to new agricultural inputs, and are then surprised when uptake stays low despite the subsidy. The three-phase model explains why: subsidies address the economics of adoption but do nothing for the cultural evaluation stage. If a technology conflicts with existing practice or belief, no amount of price support will move it past evaluation. Effective technology policy needs to budget for awareness and cultural fit, not just for the cost of the technology itself.
