# Culture and Technology in Tension

The deck frames culture as more than the arts and entertainment. It describes culture as humanity's operating system for survival, precisely because biological evolution is too slow to keep up. Culture, on this reading, includes how societies govern themselves, their social norms, customs, traditions, and political and economic systems. Viewed through this lens, culture is the mechanism that selects which technologies get taken up and which get rejected, regardless of how technically sound they are.

But the relationship runs both ways. Once a technology is adopted, it starts reshaping the culture that adopted it. The deck names one specific cost of this exchange: the devaluation of the singular human capacity to see things as a whole, in all their psychic, emotional and moral dimensions, replaced by technical calculation that is treated as superior in every respect to human judgement. This is not a claim that technical calculation is bad. It is a claim that something distinctive, judgement that holds emotional and moral weight together with technical fact, gets pushed aside when technical calculation becomes the default way of deciding things.

This is a co-evolutionary relationship rather than a one-way street. Culture determines which problems a society even thinks of as problems worth solving with technology, which is why different societies, facing broadly similar material constraints, can still end up developing quite different technologies. And the technologies a culture adopts then feed back into what that culture values and how it makes decisions, sometimes in ways nobody explicitly chose.

## Why this matters in policy

Policymakers who want to steer technological development cannot do it through technology policy alone, because technology only succeeds through cultural alignment, and culture keeps adapting in response to the technologies it takes up. Steering technology well means paying attention to the underlying cultural values that decide what problems get technological attention and what trade-offs a society is willing to accept in exchange for efficiency. Treating technology policy and cultural policy as separate domains misses how tightly the two are actually bound together.
