# Culture Definitions and Symbols

The course deck offers several overlapping definitions of culture, and it is worth holding them together rather than picking just one. Culture consists of all learned beliefs and behaviours, the rules by which a group orders its life, and the meanings its members construct to interpret their world and their place in it. It is the sum of a group's learned behaviour, generally considered its tradition, transmitted from one generation to the next. It is also a collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group from another, and a way of life accepted largely without conscious thought.

What unites these definitions is that culture is learned, not inherited biologically. Nobody is born knowing their society's rituals or values. Culture is passed along by communication and imitation, generation after generation, which is also why it can change far faster than genetics ever could.

The deck describes culture as symbolic communication. A group's skills, knowledge, attitudes, values and motives are carried in symbols, and the meanings of those symbols are learned and deliberately perpetuated through a society's institutions, not simply announced once and remembered. This matters because it means culture is transmitted through more than explicit teaching. It travels through what people do as much as through what they say.

Culture manifests in five recognisable forms: values (what a group holds important), rituals (repeated collective practices), heroes (figures who embody admired traits), symbols (words, gestures, images and objects that carry shared meaning) and practices (the visible behaviours through which the other four are enacted and observed). Practices sit closest to the surface and are the easiest to see; values sit deepest and are the hardest to change.

## Why this matters in policy

Any attempt to change behaviour at scale, whether it is a public health campaign, a tax policy or a development programme, is really an attempt to work with or against these symbolic structures. Formal announcements rarely shift deeply held values on their own, because values are transmitted through institutions and imitation, not through single messages. Policy communication that ignores rituals, heroes and symbols and speaks only in explicit statements tends to underperform policy communication that works through the channels a culture already uses to teach itself what matters.
