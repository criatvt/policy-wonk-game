# Cultural Determinism

The theory of cultural determinism holds that the ideas, meanings, beliefs and values people learn as members of a society determine human nature. People are, in a strong sense, what they learn. A direct consequence follows: there is no universal "right way" of being human. The "right way" is almost always "our way", and what counts as "our way" in one society need not correspond to "our way" in any other.

The deck presents two readings of this claim, and the difference between them matters. The optimistic version postulates that human nature is infinitely malleable, so human beings can choose the ways of life they prefer. Culture is raw material, not a cage. The pessimistic version holds the opposite: people are what they are conditioned to be, and this is something over which they have no control. On this reading, human beings are passive creatures who do whatever their culture tells them, which leads toward behaviourism, the view that locates the causes of human behaviour entirely outside the individual's control.

Cultural determinism also carries a well-known logical wrinkle. If all values are culturally conditioned, then cultural relativism, the position that no culture's values can be judged from outside, is itself a value conditioned by a particular cultural standpoint. It cannot claim universal validity without contradicting its own premise. This does not refute cultural determinism, but it does limit how far relativism can be pushed as a tool for adjudicating between cultures.

## Why this matters in policy

International policy frameworks are frequently built on claims of universal value, human rights, good governance, best practice, that assume a shared "right way." Cultural determinism explains why such frameworks routinely meet resistance: a society that sees its own norms as the natural and legitimate ones will not readily accept an external standard framed as universal, even when the external standard is well-intentioned. This does not mean policymakers should abandon cross-border standards. It means they should expect the legitimacy of those standards to be contested on cultural grounds, and should design for that contest rather than being surprised by it.
