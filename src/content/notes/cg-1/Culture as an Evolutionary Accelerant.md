# Culture as an Evolutionary Accelerant

Biological evolution works over millions of years, through the slow accumulation of genetic variation and selection. Human societies, by contrast, can change dramatically within a single generation. The course material's central move is to explain this mismatch: culture emerged as a faster substitute for biology, a way of adapting to new circumstances at the speed of learning rather than the speed of genetics.

This explains the extraordinary diversity of human cultural forms, from kinship systems to religious practice to political organisation, achieved over a span of thousands of years, not millions. Genetic variation across human populations is comparatively small; cultural variation across the same populations is vast. Biological evolution simply could not have produced this range of difference on its own. Culture had to be doing most of the work.

Read across the full historical arc, from the Big Bang to the present emergence of artificial intelligence, a pattern emerges: the pace of change keeps accelerating, and the mechanism driving that change keeps shifting away from biology. Genetic evolution gave way to cultural evolution, which is itself now shading into technological evolution, humans deliberately engineering the tools, systems and even organisms that shape what comes next. Humans have moved from being shaped by their environment to progressively becoming the authors of their own selection environment.

This is not a value judgement about whether the acceleration is good. It is a description of a trend: the more humans rely on learned, transmissible culture and technology rather than inherited genetics, the faster the rate of change becomes, and the more that change is under deliberate human control rather than blind selection.

## Why this matters in policy

If culture and technology, not biology, are now the primary drivers of human change, then policy is one of the main levers available for steering that change. This raises the stakes on questions the module returns to elsewhere: what values should guide the technologies being adopted, who gets to decide, and how much scrutiny each new technology deserves before it reshapes the culture that adopted it. Treating rapid change as something that merely happens to us, rather than something substantially produced by our own cultural and technological choices, is a category error the module is designed to correct.
