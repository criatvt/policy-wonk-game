# Layers of Culture

Culture is not a single, uniform code that everyone in a group carries identically. People, even within the same broad culture, hold several layers of mental programming at once, and these layers operate at different levels simultaneously.

The deck sets out six such layers. The national layer is the country as a whole, the broadest and most commonly assumed unit of cultural identity. The regional layer captures ethnic, linguistic and religious differences within a nation, which can be as significant as, or more significant than, differences between nations. The gender layer distinguishes female, male, gay and LGBTQ experience and expectation. The generational layer separates grandparents, parents and children, whose formative experiences and reference points differ. The social layer covers education, religion, occupation and financial capacity. The corporate layer is the particular culture of a specific organisation, which can override or interact with all the layers above it once someone is inside that organisation.

A single individual holds all six layers at once, and they do not always point in the same direction. A person's regional identity may pull one way on an issue where their generational or social identity pulls another. This is why "the national interest" or "what this country wants" is often a simplification rather than a settled fact. What actually exists, most of the time, is a contested negotiation among these overlapping layers, played out through political institutions, media and everyday argument.

## Why this matters in policy

Layered identity has direct implications for how policy is communicated and how support for policy is measured. A message calibrated only to national identity may still land badly with an audience whose regional, generational or social layer is more salient in that moment. Foreign policy built on the assumption of a single, unified national interest can also misjudge how contested that interest is domestically, since different layers of identity within the same population may genuinely want different things. Recognising the layers does not resolve the disagreement, but it explains why policy communication aimed at "the public" as a single audience routinely underperforms communication that accounts for which layer is doing the work in a given context.
