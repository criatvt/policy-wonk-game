# Geopolitics and the STRATEGY Bridge

The deck closes by situating culture and technology within statecraft. Geopolitics, in this framing, is read through a set of recurring questions: who are the actors and what interests are at stake, what is the degree of cooperation or conflict between them, how is power distributed, and what is each actor's cultural disposition, whether it leans public, strategic or military. Intelligence and informational capability sit alongside these as instruments in their own right, not as afterthoughts to military and economic power.

The deck's central device for tying this together is what it calls the STRATEGY Bridge. On one side sit interests and ends, the outcomes a state wants. Politics and policy translate those interests into direction. On the other side sit means, the instruments a state actually has available: political, military, economic and diplomatic tools, operating across a widening range of domains that now includes space, cyber, air, land and sea, in addition to the traditional battlespace.

The bridge metaphor makes a specific point: no single instrument, military strength alone, or economic leverage alone, is sufficient to connect interests to outcomes. Statecraft has become genuinely multi-domain, where informational and technological capability shape outcomes as decisively as traditional hard power once did on its own.

## Why this matters in policy

This framing explains why modern strategic competition rarely looks like a single confrontation in a single domain. A state's cultural disposition (how it sees its own strategic role), its informational reach, and its technological capability all feed into the same bridge that connects interests to outcomes, alongside its military and economic strength. Policymakers assessing a rival's or an ally's behaviour who look only at troop numbers or trade balances are reading only part of the bridge. The module's broader argument about culture and technology applies just as much here: a state's strategic behaviour is shaped by what its own culture treats as legitimate use of power, not only by its material capability.
