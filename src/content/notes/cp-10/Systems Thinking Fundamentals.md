# Systems Thinking Fundamentals

A feedback loop is a process in which A causes B, which in turn influences A, often producing further, higher-order effects the longer the loop keeps moving. In a homeostatic system, a change in one part is brought back into line by an opposite change elsewhere, the way the human body regulates its temperature or an organisation's culture resists sudden change. Homeostasis is the process by which a system self-regulates to stay within a workable range as its environment shifts. Most of the time it overshoots or undershoots slightly and has to keep correcting, the way a pilot flying a plane is off course more often than exactly on it. What matters for analysis is understanding the limits of that range, not assuming the system sits still.

A bottleneck is the point where a flow of something, tangible or not, gets constrained and stops moving continuously. Like a clogged artery or a blocked drain, a bottleneck can be small in itself but have an outsized effect if it sits on the critical path. Bottlenecks are also useful diagnostically: finding one is often the prompt to ask whether there is an alternative route to the same outcome, rather than simply pushing harder at the blockage.

Two more building blocks describe how systems respond to changes in input. The Law of Diminishing Returns holds that most real-world results see a decreasing incremental benefit past some point, and often the benefit turns negative beyond that. Giving a poor family enough money to escape poverty helps a great deal; giving them far more than that stops helping much, and can even cause harm. Critical mass, by contrast, describes a threshold at which a system jumps discretely from one phase to another rather than changing gradually, the way water abruptly turns to vapour at a specific temperature. The marginal value of the last unit before that jump is far higher than any unit before it, which is why network effects and tipping points behave so differently from steady, linear growth.

## Why this matters in policy

These five ideas are the vocabulary the rest of the module builds on. A policymaker who treats a system as a simple machine, where more input always produces proportionally more output, will misjudge both bottlenecks (where a small fix unlocks a large gain) and diminishing returns (where more spending stops helping, or starts to hurt). Recognising critical mass matters for anything that depends on adoption, from vaccination coverage to digital platforms: below the threshold, effort looks wasted; just past it, small pushes produce large effects. None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary vocabulary of noticing that systems push back, adjust, and behave differently near their limits than they do in the middle of their range.
