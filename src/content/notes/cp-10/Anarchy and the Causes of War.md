# Anarchy and the Causes of War

In international relations theory, anarchy means the absence of a legitimate governmental authority to regulate disputes and enforce agreements between states. It is the structural feature that most sharply separates international politics from domestic politics: within a state, a government can, in principle, enforce contracts and punish aggression; between states, there is no equivalent higher authority. Realists treat anarchy as the fundamental permissive cause of war. It "causes" war in the sense that nothing in the system stops it from happening, not in the sense that it makes any particular war inevitable.

Jack Levy's review of the causes-of-war literature makes a sharp logical point about this: anarchy is a structural constant, present at every point in the history of the international system, and a constant cannot explain variation. If anarchy alone were a sufficient explanation, war would be continuous, since anarchy never goes away. But peace is, empirically, far more common than war. So anarchy can answer the "permissive cause" question, why war is always possible, without answering the "explanation of variation" question, why war breaks out at some times and places and not others. Even Kenneth Waltz, the leading theorist of structural realism, effectively concedes this: neorealist theory explains war's recurrence through history but not why any particular war was fought. To explain variation, theorists have had to add further variables, such as the polarity of the system or the balance between offensive and defensive military technology.

This distinction between permissive causes and explanations of variation matters well beyond the specific debate about anarchy. It recurs, for instance, in the literature on gender and war: some feminist scholarship treats patriarchy as an alternative constant that helps explain why violence recurs in world politics, but faces the identical structural problem. Patriarchy, like anarchy, does not vary enough on its own to explain why war happens in some societies and periods and not others; it must be paired with variables that do vary if it is to generate testable predictions about when and where conflict occurs.

## Why this matters in policy

Peace policy needs both halves of the explanation. Addressing only the permissive condition, for instance by trying to build stronger international institutions to soften anarchy, does nothing about the specific triggers that turn a permissive condition into an actual war. Addressing only the specific triggers without attending to the underlying structural condition risks treating symptoms while the deeper cause remains untouched. Good conflict-prevention policy has to work at both levels at once, which is a less satisfying answer than picking one grand theory and running with it, but it is the one the evidence supports.
