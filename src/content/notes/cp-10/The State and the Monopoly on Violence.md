# The State and the Monopoly on Violence

"In Service of the Republic" opens its account of government with Max Weber's definition: a state is a human community that successfully claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. The state is often experienced as benign, almost parental, but at its core is violence. It establishes conditions in which nobody else is permitted to use force, while it retains the ability to use force itself, and it relies on the threat of that force to secure obedience on taxation and behaviour.

Every state action is grounded in this coercion, whether directly (a law that forbids an activity and threatens punishment) or indirectly (the state demands tax revenue under threat of penalty, then spends the proceeds). Gandhi's formulation, quoted in the book, is blunt: the individual has a soul, but the state is a soulless machine, and it can never be weaned away from the violence to which it owes its existence. The project of liberal democracy, on this reading, is not to pretend the state is something gentler than that. It is to limit state violence into a controlled, predictable, and just form, so that citizens consent to an ethical regime of coercion rather than an arbitrary one.

This has a direct implication for how governance should be designed. If the state is a soulless machine rather than a benevolent parent, then depending on the personal virtue of the people who happen to run it is naive. A well-intentioned official today does not guarantee a well-intentioned official tomorrow, and even well-intentioned officials operate within incentive structures that can push them towards bad outcomes regardless of character. Accountability, in this view, has to be engineered: through checks and balances, transparency, competing centres of power, and institutional design that constrains what the machine can do, rather than through appeals to the good faith of the people operating it.

## Why this matters in policy

Treating the state as a coercive machine rather than a benevolent parent changes what "good policy design" looks like. It shifts attention away from finding the right people to run a scheme and towards designing the scheme so that it works acceptably even with ordinary, self-interested people running it. Every proposal to expand what the state does is, on this framing, also a proposal to expand the domain in which private persons can be coerced, which is why the book treats the question of what the state should do at all as the first and hardest question in public policy, not an afterthought to be settled once the intervention already sounds appealing.
