# Balance of Power and Power Transition

Balance of power theory holds that the primary goal of states, especially great powers, is to prevent any single state from achieving a hegemonic position over the system. States balance against whichever power poses the greatest threat, through external alliances or internal military buildup, and balance of power theorists argue this mechanism almost always succeeds in avoiding hegemony, either because a would-be hegemon is deterred by the anticipation of a blocking coalition, or because it is defeated in the war that follows if deterrence fails. On this account, movements towards unipolarity are destabilising, because they are precisely what triggers the coalition-forming and, often, the war that restores equilibrium. Classical realists and neorealists disagree about which distribution is most stable: classical realists favour a flexible multipolar system, while neorealists such as Waltz argue bipolarity is more stable, though the evidence for this is thinner than the confidence with which it is often asserted, resting heavily on generalising from the Cold War alone.

Power transition theory offers an almost opposite account of great-power stability. It shares realism's core assumptions but emphasises hierarchy and order rather than a rough equilibrium among near-equals: hegemons arise, use their strength to build the political and economic structures that give the system stability, and stay dominant until differential growth rates, the costs of imperial overextension, and rising domestic interests elsewhere erode their lead. War is most likely not when one state is clearly dominant, and not when many states are roughly balanced, but during the dangerous transition when a rising challenger approaches parity with the declining leader. Either the challenger starts a war to bring its share of the system's benefits into line with its new strength, or the declining leader starts a preventive war to block the challenger while it still can. The resulting hegemonic war typically produces a new dominant power, and the cycle begins again.

The two theories therefore give opposite policy signals about the same situation. Balance of power theory reads a state approaching dominance as the dangerous moment, calling for balancing to restore equilibrium. Power transition theory reads the approach to parity itself as the dangerous moment, regardless of which side is balancing whom. Applying either theory to modern great-power competition also has to reckon with a variable neither fully anticipated: nuclear weapons introduce a deterrent cost to major war that may stabilise a transition period the historical pattern would otherwise flag as dangerous, though this is a live and contested question rather than a settled one.

## Why this matters in policy

Strategists reading the same rise of a challenger will draw opposite lessons depending on which theory they hold, which is a useful warning against treating either as simply true. It also illustrates a recurring theme of this module: structural theories describe the conditions under which conflict becomes more or less likely, not a mechanical prediction of what will happen, and two well-specified theories can point in genuinely different directions from the same set of facts.
