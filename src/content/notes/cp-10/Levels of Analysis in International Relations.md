# Levels of Analysis in International Relations

The causes-of-war literature is vast and often talks past itself, because different scholars are locating their explanations at different levels without saying so. The levels-of-analysis framework, following Kenneth Waltz, sorts independent variables by where they operate: the systemic level explains patterns and outcomes across the whole international system, the dyadic level explains the strategic interaction between two specific states, the national level explains the internal politics and society of a state, and the individual level explains the beliefs and choices of a particular decision-maker. Sorting variables this way prevents a specific kind of confusion, where a theory about, say, leaders' psychology is compared directly against a theory about the global distribution of power, as though the two were competing for the same explanatory job.

The framework also clarifies why it is a mistake to assume that a relationship holding at one level automatically holds at another. It is entirely possible, for instance, for concentrations of power to make peace more likely at the level of a single pair of states while making war more likely at the level of the whole system. This is the familiar problem of aggregation: correlations found at one level cannot simply be read across to another.

Levy's review also criticises the field's long-running "paradigm wars" between realism and liberalism, arguing that the framing has imposed some order on a chaotic field but at real cost. It distracts attention from the genuine variation within each paradigm, which often matters more than the label attached to a theory, and it detracts from the harder, more useful task of building specific theories and testing them against evidence. His recommendation is to shift attention from the level of paradigms, realist versus liberal, to the level of theories: construct falsifiable propositions, test them, and leave the question of which grand tradition they belong to for historians of the field to sort out later.

## Why this matters in policy

A policymaker assessing why a specific conflict is escalating benefits from knowing which level the diagnosis is actually operating at. "The system is unstable" (systemic), "these two states distrust each other specifically" (dyadic), "the leader needs a foreign crisis for domestic reasons" (national or individual) point towards very different interventions, and confusing them leads to policy aimed at the wrong lever. The broader lesson, that arguing over which grand school of thought is correct is often less productive than testing specific, falsifiable claims, travels well outside international relations, into any policy field organised around competing ideological camps rather than competing testable propositions.
