# Emergence and Policy Evaluation

Emergence describes higher-level behaviour that arises from the interaction of lower-order components. The result is frequently non-linear rather than a simple sum of the parts, and an important property of emergent behaviour is that it cannot be predicted just by studying the components in isolation. A market is not merely the sum of individual buyers and sellers; a culture is not merely the sum of individual habits. Something new appears at the system level that the component-level view does not show.

This has real consequences for how interventions are evaluated. Cost-benefit analysis, in its standard form, works by summing individual-level effects: this many people gain this much, this many lose this much, net it out. But emergent outcomes are, by definition, not reducible to that kind of sum. The interaction effects that produce a system-level result, good or bad, are invisible to any analysis that only looks at component-level impacts. This is one reason government interventions so often generate unintended consequences worse than the original problem: a linear policy analysis misses the feedback effects and emergent shifts that a genuinely complex system produces in response to being poked.

A related idea is irreducibility. Most systems have quantitative properties, complexity, minimums, time, and length, below which the desired result simply does not occur. You cannot get several women pregnant to reduce the time needed to have one child, and you cannot build a working car out of a single part. Applied to institutions, this means there are minimum thresholds of institutional quality, legal infrastructure, or fiscal resources below which governance functions simply fail. A court system funded at half the level it needs is not half as effective; below some floor, it may not function as a court system at all.

## Why this matters in policy

Emergence and irreducibility together argue for humility in how policy is evaluated and designed. Evaluating only direct, first-order effects will systematically miss the system-level consequences that only appear once a policy interacts with the wider system it sits inside, for better or worse. And partial investment below an irreducible threshold can look like progress on a spreadsheet while producing nothing that actually works on the ground. Neither point argues against intervention. It argues for evaluating at the level where the effect actually shows up, and for taking "we are almost at the threshold" as a warning rather than as good news.
