# Free Markets and Self-Organisation

"In Service of the Republic" argues that before deciding what the state should do, it is worth appreciating what free people achieve without it. In a competitive market, firms are given no guarantee of survival or profit, so they are put on a permanent treadmill: energetically looking for ways to please customers better and produce more cheaply, because a firm that fails to do so eventually loses out to one that does. This ceaseless pressure to improve, driven purely by firms and consumers pursuing their own interests, generates rising productivity, and those productivity gains flow to workers as higher wages. No central planner has to design this outcome or coordinate it; it emerges from the ordinary self-interest of many independent actors responding to prices and competition.

The book's recurring theme is the strength of self-organising systems: the uncoordinated decisions of many individuals, left to themselves, discovering an order that nobody designed. It illustrates this with the early history of computer networking. A global consortium of governments developed and backed a networking standard called X.25. An informal, loosely coordinated group of academics and hobbyists, with no state backing at all, developed an alternative called TCP/IP. TCP/IP won. It became the protocol underlying the modern Internet, while the government-backed X.25 fell away. The lesson the book draws is not that governments are always wrong about technology, but that organic, self-organising systems can out-coordinate deliberately planned ones, even without the resources and authority a state can bring to bear, because they harness dispersed knowledge and individual initiative that no central authority can fully replicate or replace.

This is offered as a caution, not an absolute rule: the book pairs it with an explicit account of market failure, where the free market predictably underperforms and coercive state action has legitimate ground to intervene. The claim is narrower and more useful than "markets always win": self-organising systems tend to outperform centrally planned alternatives specifically because they aggregate information and initiative that a planner cannot access, which is a mechanism, not a slogan.

## Why this matters in policy

The instinct to solve a coordination problem by having the state design and impose a single, uniform solution is often the wrong first move. Before reaching for a mandate or a state-run alternative, it is worth asking whether a decentralised process, private standards bodies, market competition, voluntary coordination, might solve the same problem while making use of information the state does not have. That question does not settle every case in favour of the market. It just makes sure the comparison is fair, rather than assuming central coordination wins by default.
