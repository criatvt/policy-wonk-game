# Systems thinking for policy

This module combines three threads. The first is systems thinking: the mental models that explain how complex systems behave, from feedback loops to emergence to bottlenecks, drawn from Farnam Street's primer on the subject. The second is the political economy of the state, taken from "In Service of the Republic": what governments are for, why intervention is hard, and when self-organising systems outperform planned ones. The third is the structural conditions of conflict between states, drawn from Jack Levy's 1998 review of the causes-of-war literature. The throughline is that the world is full of complex adaptive systems, and treating them as if they were simple machines tends to produce policy that doesn't work.

Topics in this module:

- **Systems Thinking Fundamentals**: feedback loops, bottlenecks, diminishing returns, critical mass, and homeostasis.
- **Feedback Loops and Resilience at Scale**: runaway feedback, churn, why systems behave differently at scale, and margins of safety.
- **Emergence and Policy Evaluation**: why the whole is not the sum of its parts, and what that means for evaluating interventions.
- **The State and the Monopoly on Violence**: Weber's definition of the state and what it implies for accountability.
- **Anarchy and the Causes of War**: why a structural constant cannot explain variation in war and peace.
- **Levels of Analysis in International Relations**: organising the causes-of-war literature by what level a variable operates at.
- **Realism and Neorealism**: classical realism, Waltz's structural turn, and what structural theories can and cannot explain.
- **Balance of Power and Power Transition**: two competing structural accounts of when great-power wars happen.
- **The Security Dilemma**: why defensive moves can still spiral into conflict.
- **Free Markets and Self-Organisation**: why decentralised coordination often beats central planning.
- **The Limits of Government Intervention**: what the humble charger-standardisation problem reveals about state capacity.
- **The Art and Science of Policy Design**: when analysis is enough, and when policy requires judgement.
