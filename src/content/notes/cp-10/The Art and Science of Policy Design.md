# The Art and Science of Policy Design

"In Service of the Republic" distinguishes between the science of public policy and its art. The science covers the decisions with strong analytical foundations and reasonably solid empirical evidence, such as identifying a market failure using the standard categories of externalities, asymmetric information, market power, and public goods. The art covers everything beyond that, where the evidence runs out and policymakers have to form judgements that may reasonably differ from one thoughtful person to the next, feeling their way through intuition and experience rather than reading an answer off a model.

Redistribution is offered as a clear case where the underlying goal, helping the poorest, commands broad normative support, and yet implementation is "fraught with trouble" for reasons that are more art than science. Redistribution disturbs incentives on both the giving and receiving side. It requires accurately identifying who the intended beneficiaries actually are, which demands a level of state capacity that is often simply absent, especially at the scale needed: transferring even a modest daily amount to India's poorest population runs into the trillions of rupees a year, and correctly identifying that population is itself a serious administrative challenge. And it generates political resistance from whoever bears the cost. None of this means redistribution is wrong. It means the case for it being normatively desirable does not, by itself, make it easy or safe to design well.

A second test case is whether a policy stance can coherently endorse free markets and government intervention at the same time. The book argues yes, provided the two are matched correctly: markets are treated as presumptively efficient and left alone where they are, while genuine market failures, the kind with real analytical grounding, are targeted with intervention suited to that specific failure. This is coherent because it is not "markets, except when I personally dislike the outcome"; it is a principled division of labour between the two which is exactly what the science-versus-art distinction is meant to formalise: use the science where it applies, use judgement matched to a real, identified problem everywhere else.

## Why this matters in policy

The science/art distinction is a discipline as much as a description. It pushes analysts to be honest about which parts of a proposal rest on solid evidence and which rest on judgement calls dressed up as certainty, and it explains why two policy experts who agree on the underlying economics can still land on different recommendations. Recognising redistribution and mixed market-and-intervention stances as legitimate but genuinely hard "art" cases, rather than either obviously right or obviously ideological, is a more honest way to hold the debate.
