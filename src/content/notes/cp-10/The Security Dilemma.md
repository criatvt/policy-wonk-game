# The Security Dilemma

Robert Jervis's concept of the security dilemma, drawn on heavily in the causes-of-war literature, captures a genuinely structural problem in an anarchic system: the actions a state takes to make itself more secure can make every state, including itself, less secure. A government builds up its military purely for defensive reasons, with no intention of aggression. But other states cannot directly observe intentions, only capabilities, and a defensive buildup looks identical, from the outside, to preparation for attack. Adversaries respond with buildups of their own, which the first state then reads as confirmation of the threat it feared, and the spiral continues. Nobody in this sequence needs to want war for the interaction to produce one.

This is why the dilemma is genuinely hard to escape, and not simply a failure of communication or goodwill between states. There is no credible way for a state to prove, through words alone, that its buildup is purely defensive; assurances are cheap and unverifiable, while the capability itself is what an adversary must plan against. Realist scholars, recognising that international politics is not permanently and uniformly conflictual, have looked at the conditions under which cooperation becomes possible under anarchy despite this dilemma, often using game-theoretic models to formalise the problem.

One important refinement is the choice of game used to model the interaction. Much of the classical analysis borrowed the Prisoner's Dilemma, in which each side's individually rational move is to defect regardless of what the other side does. But Levy's review notes that a stag hunt may often be the more accurate model: in a stag hunt, both sides' first preference is mutual cooperation, and the risk is not that either side wants to defect, but that each fears the other will defect first, or fears being caught unprepared if the other strikes pre-emptively. That distinction matters enormously for policy, because it implies that many real-world conflicts arise less from states wanting war and more from coordination failures and mutual misperception, which point towards confidence-building measures and verification rather than towards the deterrence-heavy prescriptions a pure Prisoner's Dilemma framing would suggest.

## Why this matters in policy

Arms control, transparency measures, and verification regimes exist precisely because the security dilemma cannot be resolved by intentions alone; it has to be addressed through mechanisms that make capabilities and behaviour observable and costly to fake. Recognising whether a specific standoff looks more like a Prisoner's Dilemma or a stag hunt changes what kind of diplomacy is likely to help, which is exactly the sort of distinction that gets lost when "security dilemma" is used loosely as a synonym for "states don't trust each other."
