# Feedback Loops and Resilience at Scale

Not every feedback loop restores balance. A runaway feedback loop is one where the output of a reaction becomes its own catalyst, a process called autocatalysis. Where a homeostatic loop corrects a deviation and pulls the system back towards equilibrium, a runaway loop amplifies the deviation and drives the system further from where it started. Bank runs, viral misinformation, and arms races behave this way: each round of the loop makes the next round larger, not smaller.

Churn is a related but distinct idea. Insurance companies and subscription services track it closely: every year, a certain share of customers is lost and must be replaced before any net growth happens at all. Standing still is the equivalent of losing ground, a pattern sometimes called the Red Queen Effect. Many organisations and institutions have a churn rate of their own, whether it is staff, members, or public trust, and a system that ignores its own churn will eventually discover that its apparent stability was really just its replacement rate keeping pace with its losses.

Systems are also sensitive to scale: properties and behaviours that hold at one size often do not hold when the system grows or shrinks by an order of magnitude. A management structure, a distribution network, or a subsidy scheme that works cleanly for a pilot of a few thousand people can behave in entirely different, non-proportional ways once it covers hundreds of millions. Any serious analysis of a complex system has to keep asking what scale it is being observed at, rather than assuming small-scale intuitions travel unchanged.

Finally, engineers build a margin of safety into their calculations as a matter of habit. A bridge rated to hold 9,600 pounds is not built to carry exactly that much traffic; it is built with slack, because the world is unpredictable and few modern bridges fail as a result. The same logic applies well beyond engineering.

## Why this matters in policy

Institutions designed only for expected conditions tend to fail under unexpected stress, precisely because they were optimised for the average case rather than built with tolerance for error. A margin of safety in public institutions looks like redundancy, reserve capacity, and slack that seems wasteful in normal times, right up until the moment it is the only thing standing between a shock and a collapse. Recognising runaway loops and churn early, and building in room for error before a crisis, is cheaper than discovering the absence of both after one has already started. Which is, admittedly, easier to write in a revision note than to fund in an annual budget.
