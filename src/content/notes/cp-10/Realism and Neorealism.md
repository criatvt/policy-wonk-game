# Realism and Neorealism

Realism has dominated the study of interstate war since Thucydides, but it is not one theory. Classical realists, in the tradition of Hans Morgenthau, explain conflict through a combination of the inherently aggressive tendencies of human nature and the absence of any higher authority above states. Kenneth Waltz's neorealism, developed from the late 1970s, drops human nature as an explanatory concept almost entirely. It gives primacy instead to the anarchic structure of the international system, retains the classical assumption that states are the central actors and behave rationally, and builds a structural-systemic theory in which state behaviour is explained by a state's position within the system rather than by the character of its leaders.

This structural move has a documented cost. Because neorealism treats anarchy as a constant, it inherits the same problem covered elsewhere in this module: a constant cannot explain variation. Waltz himself effectively concedes that his theory explains why war recurs across history but not why any specific war happened, and later neorealists have had to smuggle in additional variables, such as the polarity of the system (bipolar versus multipolar) or the offense-defense balance, to get any purchase on variation at all. That is a genuine tension for a theory that presents itself as purely structural: the moment it needs auxiliary variables to explain outcomes, its claim to structural purity is complicated.

What structural theories of this kind, neorealism, balance of power, power transition, and the security dilemma, collectively offer is an account of the landscape: which conditions make conflict more or less likely across cases and over time. What they do not offer, and do not claim to offer on their own terms, is an explanation of why a specific leader chose war at a specific moment. Explaining Saddam Hussein's decision to invade Kuwait in August 1990 requires descending to the level of that particular decision-maker's beliefs, information, and calculations. Structural theory sets the stage; it does not write the specific scene.

## Why this matters in policy

Structural realism is useful for identifying which international configurations are more conflict-prone in general, which is valuable for long-run strategic planning. It is a poor tool for predicting or explaining a specific crisis, which needs finer-grained, lower-level analysis instead. Policymakers who reach for a single grand theory to explain a live crisis are usually reaching for the wrong tool, however tidy the theory is.
